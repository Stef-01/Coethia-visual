Faster Than the Rumour

Measles Came Back Because a Number Slipped From 95 to 92.4. What That Costs, What the Feed Is Actually Saying, and Why the Correction Never Arrives in Time

In February 2025 an unvaccinated six-year-old died in a hospital in Lubbock, Texas — the first measles death in the United States in a decade. By August 2026 the country had recorded 2,465 confirmed cases, the most since 1991, and its measles elimination status was scheduled for review.

This piece is written from the chair of the person who has to do something about that: a county health officer with a statutory mandate, a shrinking budget, and no way to be present in the room where the decision actually gets made — which is a phone, at three in the morning, six weeks before the appointment.

It starts in the hospital room and ends at an operating model. Nothing is being sold until you have been given the reason.

The Narrow, Defensible Claim

Better advertising would not have saved the child in Lubbock. Her parents were asked, afterwards, whether it had changed their minds about vaccination, and they said it had not. That is a settled commitment, and settled commitments are not moved by a better-targeted video.

The claim this piece makes is narrower than that, and it survives the objection. Ninety-three percent of the cases in this outbreak were unvaccinated or of unknown status — and that population is not one belief. It contains the family nobody ever asked, the shift worker whose clinic closes at four, the parent on an alternative schedule who still intends to finish it, and the person genuinely unable to adjudicate between two confident pages. Four groups out of five, and four groups that are the majority.

Nobody reached them, because nobody was there when they were deciding. Everything after the fortieth scene is about what being there would actually require.

Start With the Listening

The cheapest version of this is two weeks and no campaign attached: what your county is actually searching, which beliefs are load-bearing, and where the access gaps sit. It is the least expensive way to find out whether the rest of it is worth buying — and it is a fraction of the $244,480 it costs to open an outbreak response before the second case is even counted.

What Is Real and What Is Illustrative

The epidemiology, the coverage data, the costs and the capacity figures are real and cited. Case counts, deaths, kindergarten MMR coverage and exemption rates come from CDC; R₀ and the herd-immunity threshold from the published systematic review; outbreak response costs from the Johns Hopkins scaling study and the New Mexico cost analysis in Annals of Internal Medicine; workforce and budget figures from NACCHO and Trust for America’s Health. Sources sit beneath the scenes that use them.

The two children are real. The February 2025 death and Daisy Hildebrand’s death in April 2025 are matters of public record, reported by Texas DSHS and covered contemporaneously. They are named because rounding them into “cases” is exactly the habit this piece is arguing against.

The feed is a reconstruction. The reels, handles, captions and comment threads are composed for this piece in the genre’s real visual grammar. They are not screenshots of identifiable accounts, and no real creator is depicted. The engagement asymmetry they illustrate is documented; the specific posts are not.

The client is composite and the console is synthetic. “Marion County Public Health” is invented to carry the walkthrough, and every metric inside the recreated Google Ads console — audience sizes, costs per action, comprehension lifts — is generated in this file. The platform mechanics around them are real and documented: Ad Grants terms, custom-segment inputs, the health-condition targeting restrictions, Ad Rank arithmetic, placement targeting and the privacy constraints on health data.

Coethia is a proposed system, not a shipped product, and the evidence on advertising effectiveness is genuinely mixed. Where the research supports a claim it is cited; where effects are variable or nonsignificant, the piece says so rather than quoting the best number.
